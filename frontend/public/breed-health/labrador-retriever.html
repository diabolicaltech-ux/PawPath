Labrador Retriever Health Guide

A breed page can help you know what to ask about, but it cannot predict your individual dog’s health. A breed tendency is not a diagnosis. These are possible breed-related things to discuss with your veterinarian, not predictions. Your veterinarian can consider the breed alongside your dog’s examination, history, daily life, and current signs.

Breed Overview

The approved Labrador Retriever profile describes this breed as originating in Newfoundland, Canada, where Labradors worked with fishermen to retrieve nets and fish. The profile lists their historical purpose as fishing and retrieving companion for fishermen and notes development in the nineteenth century. Labradors are active, people-oriented dogs who often enjoy swimming, fetch, retrieving games, hiking, running, agility, and scent work. Your dog does not need every activity on that list. Ask your veterinarian how to choose exercise and enrichment that fit your dog’s health, fitness, environment, and interests. Breed history is useful context, not a health forecast. Your dog’s ancestry, body condition, life stage, previous medical history, medications, and home environment all matter too.

Common Health Considerations

The approved profile lists these possible breed-related concerns:

  • Obesity (carrying excess body fat): Labradors may be prone to weight gain. Ask your veterinarian how to choose food, treats, portions, and activity for your dog.
  • Hip and elbow dysplasia (abnormal joint development): This can be a breed-related orthopedic concern. Ask your veterinarian how to support comfortable movement and body condition.
  • Ear infections (irritation or infection in the ear canal): A dog who swims may need an individualized ear-care plan. Ask your veterinarian what to watch for and whether cleaning is appropriate.
  • Exercise-induced collapse, or EIC (an inherited tendency toward weakness or collapse after activity): Ask your veterinarian how to plan safe activity and what to do if your dog shows concerning changes.

These are possible breed-related things to discuss, not predictions. Your dog may never have any of them, and other health concerns may matter more. Do not change medication, food, exercise, supplements, or preventive care based only on a breed page. Ask your veterinarian which examinations or evaluations fit your dog.

Nutrition & Weight

There is no universal portion or calorie target that fits every Labrador. Food energy, activity, life stage, health conditions, medicines, treats, and current body condition all affect an appropriate plan. Ask your veterinarian how to select food, measure portions, and monitor your dog’s body condition. A healthy plan can change over time. Record food details, treats, weight observations, activity, medications, allergies, and questions in PawPath so you can share a clear history with your veterinary team. If appetite, digestion, energy, hydration, movement, or weight changes, contact your veterinarian. Do not use this page to prescribe a diet, supplement, restriction, or feeding routine.

Preventive Care

Preventive care should fit your individual dog. Ask your veterinarian how visits, vaccines, parasite prevention, dental care, grooming, activity, nutrition, and body-condition monitoring should change as your dog grows and ages. Bring prior records, current medicines and supplements, and changes in routine to appointments. Your veterinarian may discuss orthopedic, ear, eye, genetic, or other evaluations when appropriate. There is no universal screening schedule for every Labrador. Ask which examinations or tests make sense for your dog’s history, signs, and lifestyle.

Living With This Breed

A simple record of what is normal for your dog can make changes easier to spot. Note appetite, energy, movement, breathing, ears, skin and coat, digestion, urination, sleep, and behavior. Share meaningful changes with your veterinary team rather than comparing your dog with another Labrador. Labradors often enjoy active play, but the right pace and surfaces depend on the individual dog. Ask your veterinarian about exercise, swimming safety, enrichment, transport, restraint, grooming, dental care, and comfortable rest. When to See a Veterinarian

Contact your veterinary team about concerning changes in breathing, movement, appetite, digestion, energy, ears, skin or coat, urination, behavior, or comfort. Do not attempt to diagnose or treat a suspected breed condition at home. For any pet, severe or rapidly worsening signs require emergency care — contact your veterinarian or an emergency veterinary hospital immediately.
